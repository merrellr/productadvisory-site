# Part 1: Foundations

# 1. The Product Leader's Job, Now

**The big idea.** The product leader owns the outcome: whether the product creates value for customers and the business, and whether the team is working on the right problem. AI now drafts much of the work, which makes human judgment more important, not less.

**Key points**

- Every product decision answers five questions: is it **valuable, usable, feasible, viable and trustworthy?** Trust is new in this edition.
- The role evolved from feature coordinator, to backlog owner, to outcome owner, and now to **orchestrator** of people and AI agents.
- Four pillars support the job: **market understanding, financial insight, customer-centered innovation, and trust and responsibility.**
- Great product leaders state the problem in one sentence, talk to customers weekly, separate the request from the need, write decisions down, and say no kindly.
- AI removes toil and raises the bar on judgment. **Delegate the first draft, never the final call.**
- Time that AI frees should go to customers, decisions and the team, not to more meetings.

![The four pillars of product leadership.](../fig/1-1.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Can you state the outcome your product is accountable for in one sentence?
- [ ] Have you answered all five questions for your top priority?
- [ ] Which pillar is your weakest, and what will you do about it?
- [ ] Do you keep a decision log the team can read?
- [ ] Where are you relying on AI output without reading the evidence behind it?

**Try this week.** Sort a week of your calendar into customer understanding, decisions, team leadership and coordination. Pick five hours of coordination to hand to an AI assistant, and protect those hours for customers.

# 2. Finding Problems Worth Solving

**The big idea.** Products rarely fail because they could not be built. They fail because they solved a problem nobody valued enough. Find the problem, the people who have it and the people who pay, before you design anything.

**Key points**

- Write a **problem statement** that names who, their goal, the obstacle, the consequence and today's workaround, with no solution in it.
- Look for the **job to be done:** the functional, emotional and social progress people are trying to make.
- In business software the user, buyer and decision-maker differ. Map the **buying group** and what each role needs to believe.
- Score problems by **how often they happen and how much they hurt.** Build where both are high.
- Size the market **bottom up,** from countable customers and a realistic price.
- Your biggest competitor is usually **the status quo:** the spreadsheet, the phone call, doing nothing.
- Learn the regulatory, data and integration constraints first; they shape the solution.

![Opportunity map: build where problems are frequent and painful (illustrative).](../fig/2-3.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Does your problem statement name a specific person and contain no solution?
- [ ] Have you mapped the buying group and what each role needs to believe?
- [ ] Is your market estimate built from numbers you could verify?
- [ ] Have you listed the status quo and "do nothing" as competitors?
- [ ] Have you seen the problem in the field, not just in desk research?

**Try this week.** Show your problem statement to a customer, a seller and an engineer, and ask each, "What is wrong with this?" Rewrite it after every conversation.

# 3. Discovery and Validation, from Interviews to MVP

**The big idea.** Discovery is a weekly habit, not a project phase. Test the riskiest assumptions cheaply, match the strength of evidence to the size of the decision, and build the smallest product that proves real value.

**Key points**

- The product trio should talk with customers **every week.**
- Interview about **specific past events,** not opinions about your idea. Follow the workarounds. Talk to people who said no.
- List assumptions across desirability, usability, feasibility, viability and trust. **Test the riskiest first.**
- Climb the **evidence ladder** only as high as the decision needs: interviews, prototypes, concierge and Wizard of Oz tests, then pilots and pre-orders.
- An MVP is the smallest thing that **delivers real value** and tests the core assumption. Write down what it leaves out.
- Pilots need a measure, baseline, threshold, duration and **named decision-maker,** agreed in writing.
- AI is excellent at synthesis. Humans own the conversations and conclusions. **Synthetic users are not evidence.**

![The evidence ladder.](../fig/3-1.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Does your trio talk with customers every week?
- [ ] Have you tested your riskiest assumption first?
- [ ] Can you state your MVP in one sentence, with a list of what it leaves out?
- [ ] Does every pilot end in a decision?
- [ ] Is every AI tool that touches customer data approved for it?

**Try this week.** Pick the assumption with the highest importance and least evidence, and design an experiment that could disprove it within two weeks.
