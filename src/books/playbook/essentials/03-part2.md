# Part 2: Strategy

# 4. Vision, Strategy and Positioning

**The big idea.** Strategy is a set of choices: a clear diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions, including what you will stop doing. Positioning helps customers understand those choices.

**Key points**

- Keep the cascade straight: **vision** (why), **strategy** (where to play, how to win), **outcomes** (how we'll know), **roadmap** (what we'll try), **backlog** (what we're building).
- A good vision describes **the customer's world,** not your product, and is concrete enough to rule things out.
- Good strategy has a **diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions** (Rumelt).
- **Where to play** and **how to win** must fit each other (Lafley and Martin).
- Build positioning **from the competitive alternatives outward,** and choose the category last (Dunford).
- Fit the strategy on **one page,** and repeat it until people can repeat it back.
- AI commoditizes features. Durable advantage comes from workflow depth, data, trust and relationships.

![The strategy cascade.](../fig/4-1.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Is your vision one sentence about the customer's world?
- [ ] Does your strategy contain a diagnosis, policy and actions?
- [ ] Does your positioning start from what customers would do without you?
- [ ] Is there a written, shared not-doing list?
- [ ] Could a competitor with the same AI tools copy your advantage in a quarter?

**Try this week.** Write your strategy on one page. Ask a new engineer to name two ideas it rules out. If they can't, rewrite it.

# 5. Prioritization and Outcome Roadmaps

**The big idea.** Roadmaps communicate direction, not promises. Frame work as outcomes, show confidence honestly, and make the trade-offs visible, including the capacity that keeps the product healthy.

**Key points**

- **Outcomes over output:** an outcome is a change in customer behavior that drives results.
- Use **Now, Next, Later:** problems and measures, with detail and confidence thinning from left to right, and dates only where they are real.
- Frameworks such as **RICE, weighted scoring, Kano and cost of delay** structure the conversation. They do not make the decision, and each has biases.
- When two methods disagree sharply, **discuss the item** instead of trusting a formula.
- Say no **with reasons and an alternative.**
- **Decide the capacity split first:** new outcomes, reliability, security, debt and discovery.
- Archive backlog items no current customer problem supports.

![An outcome roadmap: Now, Next, Later.](../fig/5-1.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Is every roadmap item a problem or outcome, with a measure?
- [ ] Are dates shown only for real commitments?
- [ ] Do you know your prioritization framework's biases?
- [ ] Is capacity for reliability and security set before features compete?
- [ ] Do AI items have quality targets, not just ship dates?

**Try this week.** For every roadmap item, write the problem and the measure. Anything without both goes to a "needs a problem" list.

# 6. The Numbers: P&L, Unit Economics and Pricing

**The big idea.** Every product decision is a financial decision. Know your gross margin, your cost to serve, your payback and your retention, and price to the value customers receive.

**Key points**

- Read the **P&L:** revenue, cost of revenue, gross margin, operating expenses, operating income.
- **Gross margin** is the clearest sign of whether software is a good business. Custom work and AI costs erode it.
- Track **CAC, payback, gross and net revenue retention, and LTV to CAC.**
- Some customers cost more to serve than they pay. **Find them.**
- Pricing is the most powerful lever: a **1 percent price improvement** raised operating profit by about 11 percent in a classic study of 2,463 companies.
- Price to **value,** choose a **pricing metric** that grows with value, and package deliberately.
- Business cases need the **cost to run,** not just the cost to build, and a range rather than one number.

![Price is the most powerful profit lever. Source: Marn and Rosiello, Harvard Business Review, 1992.](../fig/6-3.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Do you know your gross margin and what drives it?
- [ ] Do you know your cost to serve a typical customer, including AI?
- [ ] Is your price based on customer value?
- [ ] Does your pricing metric grow with the value you deliver?
- [ ] Do proposals include running costs and a range?

**Try this week.** Calculate revenue, cost to serve, gross margin, acquisition cost and payback for your typical, smallest and most demanding customers. Compare them.

# 7. Managing a Product Portfolio

**The big idea.** With more than one product, the question changes from "what next?" to "how much should each get, and what should stop?" Manage each product for its stage, and fund the future as well as the present.

**Key points**

- Products move through **introduction, growth, maturity and decline.** Measure each on what matters at its stage.
- A **portfolio map** (growth, margin, revenue) turns separate pitches into one shared picture.
- Fund **three horizons:** the core, emerging businesses and future options. Roughly 70/20/10 is a common guideline.
- Build **platform** capabilities from real needs of two or more products, not speculation.
- **Bundle only after integrating** identity, data and design.
- **Retire products well:** reasons, notice, migration, careful data handling, and redeployed capacity.
- Portfolio reviews should end with **decisions,** not status updates.

![Three horizons of growth.](../fig/7-2.png){width=100%}

**Checklist**

- [ ] Is each product measured for its life-cycle stage?
- [ ] Does leadership discuss a shared portfolio map?
- [ ] Is the horizon split explicit?
- [ ] Do you have a responsible retirement plan for declining products?
- [ ] Have you asked whether an AI-native approach could replace your core?

**Try this week.** Plot your products or modules by growth and margin, label each stage, and write one word for each: invest, hold, fix or retire.
